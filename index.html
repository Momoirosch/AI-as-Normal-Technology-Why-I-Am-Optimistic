A personal reflection on artificial intelligence

AI as Normal Technology – Why I Am Optimistic

When people talk about artificial intelligence, the discussion often leaps towards two extreme directions. AI is either a technology that will solve most problems, creating an utopia, or as something that will eventually become so powerful, to the point where humanity loses control over it. In AI as Normal Technology, Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor argue for a less dramatic view. They compare it to earlier general-purpose technologies like the internet or electricity. As something that can change the world/society, but is still being developed, taking its time to integrate.

I enjoyed their perspective and think it’s convincing. I would say I am very positive about AI and technological progress in general, so I am obviously not completely unbiased on the topic. Yet I still think the authors make a good case that AI will most likely change society at a much lower rate and in a less graceful way than some predictions suggest.

Thesis 1: AI will probably progress like other technologies, but humans still need to decide on how trivial the mistakes are

The argument I agreed with most, is the distinction between what AI is capable of doing and how quickly those capabilities can actually be adopted by or integrated into society. While models can evolve and improve within months, companies and the government can not adjust to it that fast. People need training, workflows have to change, and systems need to show that they are reliable enough to be trusted. This is especially important in areas where a mistake could cause major and/or serious consequences.

A recent example shows us how dangerous it can be when such verification is missing. CNN reported that a United States military intelligence report created with the help of AI incorrectly identified the cargo of a Chinese ship to be components for a nuclear weapon. MIlitary aircraft were reportedly already in air and the soldiers were preparing for a possible boarding operation before people analysed the information at which point they discovered that chatbot had misidentified the cargo CNN Transcripts

To me, this is a shocking but good example of why AI should not simply be trusted, its output can sound convincing but can be just hallucinated. This is something that is usually just annoying in normal conversation with AI, but depending on where it is used, it can have enormous consequences.

A friend I recently heard of again, told me about a company that was developing highly complex simulation systems. According to him after months of work they discovered that the system they were developing with the help of AI produced results that looked as if it did simulations, but turned out to be just faking them. I can not verify that example, but I think the lesson that can be learned from it is important. If something is important or even paramount to a project, it should be tested rather than accepted. Just because output looks plausible does not mean it is correct.

Thesis 2: Strong defenses are more useful than simply restricting our own AI capabilities

I also agree with the article's argument that many risks should be addressed where actual harm occurs, instead of trying to limit the capabilities of the AI.

This is illustrated well by the phishing example they provide. Writing a professional or convincing email is not inherently malicious. Yes it may be used for fraud, but it is also used for normal communication and marketing. So reducing the capability of the AI to produce convincing text would remove the legitimate ways in which it is used. The authors argue that defences such as spam mail detection in this case, or in general things like: access control, cybersecurity, and monitoring are often more useful as they target the point at which harm occurs.

In my opinion the same principle applies on a larger scale. Some regulations make sense, especially in areas where AI is used in medicine, military decisionmaking or other high-risk areas. But limiting our progress, restricting or slowing it down, does not prevent threats, or makes us safer. Other countries, companies or malicious groups may continue developing those capabilities.

If one side heavily restricts their own development while others do not, they might only make themselves less capable, while at the same time also having less knowledge of how good others can perform, making them less capable of responding later. I would therefore prefer investment in strong defensive systems and sensible safeguards, over relying mainly on limiting what our own AI can do. As AI improves offensive cybersecurity for example, we should also use it to improve its defensive capabilities, like vulnerability detection, fraud prevention, and defensive cybersecurity.

This does not mean that all regulation is bad. The important distinction, in my opinion, is between restrictions that make dangerous deployment safer and restrictions which just slow down the development, without removing the risk that is actually involved in it.

Thesis 3: The benefits of AI justify continued development, even tho the consequences are not nonexistent

One reason why I am optimistic about AI is the various examples where it has already contributed with major effects. AlphaFold is one of those examples I favor a lot. It’s an AI model for protein.structure prediction, which allowed it to find the 3-dimensional structure of proteins way faster than it was done earlier, solving a problem researchers have been working on for decades. This contribution even gave the people behind AlphaFold: Demis Hassabis and John Jumper, half of the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Nobel Prize

It’s exactly because of examples like this, that excite me about the possibility of AI in science, medicine, programming, and many other fields

At the same time, technological progress also creates real social problems. Employment is one such example, that is and has been in the news for a while. May it be companies letting go of many employees and replacing them with AI, or artists struggling to find work with the spread of AI art.

I could easily imagine certain jobs where many people doing similar work, will be replaced by fewer, maybe even just one person, overseeing the work AI does in their place. At the same time I also expect companies to go too far and later discover that they removed too many employees. So I expect the AI adoption to be messy and involve a lot of experimentation rather than one clean transition.

AI-generated art is another example where I see both sides. I have dabbled in drawing myself, and something I repeatedly heard was the term “Stealing like an artist”. What they meant is to take things you like from certain art, and develop it into your own style. After all, art has always evolved through inspiration from nature or from earlier art.

This is why I find it somewhat strange when people call AI art stealing. I get it when they argue about the sourcing of the art. But I think it is strange to say, when people get inspired by art it is okay, but when AI does it, it’s considered stealing. That wording can also make it sound as if image generation would just photoshop parts from old pictures into new artworks. That is not normally how diffusion models work. They learn patterns and representations from training data, generating new images from the learnt underlying structures.

This does not mean that artists have no reason to be concerned. As mentioned above the question of sourcing training data is a valid point they bring up. Also places where AI art is used to mimic the specific style of an artist without transforming it, is a valid complaint.

Google’s Gemini image generator has shown another side of the same problem. Google tried to make its outputs more diverse, but its adjustments were overcompensating. When people prompted for historical figures, or groups like vikings, the generator produced images of people of colour, even when there was neither a specific prompt for such, nor a historical reason for it. Google shortly after paused its image generation for people, acknowledging that the system had issues. blog.google

Examples like this show me that new systems will make mistakes, but the important thing is to identify them, understand them and improve the technology, instead of expecting it to be perfect, or fearing mistakes.

Where do I see AI in five years

I do not expect there to be a superintelligent AI controlling society in five years, nor an utopia. I expect AI to be much more deeply integrated into normal work as it already is.

While programming, customer service and research already work with AI in a noticeable way now, I assume it will be way more involved and also be more commonplace in office work and content creation. Some jobs will without a doubt require less people, and some workfields might no longer exist.. This however will lead to more positions in supervising AI, working on the parts the AI can not do yet and prompting them.

I also expect robotics to improve. We already have a lot of dynamic reactions of robots to the environment through the advancements of machine learning and its use in controlling robots. But I think physical automation will remain slower than purely digital AI, as it is much harder to control than a computer environment

In the much longer term, I can imagine advanced AI combined with robotics replacing a large portion of today’s human workforce. At that point the main concern would no longer be how "intelligent" the AI is, but how much physical and economic power it has. Showing a strong connection to the author's distinction between capability and power.

Overall I enjoyed AI as Normal Technology because it offers an alternative to both extreme optimism and overwhelming fear. I also enjoy speculative topics such as simulation theory, or Dyson spheres, but they also serve as a reminder on how difficult predicting the future is.

I am clearly biased towards technological progress because I find it fascinating what technology allows us to do. At the same time, being optimistic about AI should not mean to blindly trust it. For me the best path would be to continue developing AI, carefully verify it, pay attention to mistakes, build strong defenses against misuse and learn what the technology can actually do.

This seems much closer to treating AI as a normal technology, neither harmless, or magical, nor predetermined.
